The Uncomfortable Room

On the Freud you don't study for the exam

Carlos Eduardo Ravello Joo · 30 September 2026 · 10 min read

There are two Freuds.

One is the Freud you study and, above all, the one who fascinates people outside the lecture hall. The Freud of the psychosexual stages, the Oedipus complex, the unconscious as a storeroom for whatever was repressed. He gets memorised, repeated, and every so often someone makes a film about him. And yes: he has aged badly.

The other is hardly ever taught. He is hard to recognise as theory — not because he isn't, but because Freud was turned into an exam character. Into a multiple-choice question. Into classroom projects built on intrusive questionnaires that should have been retired decades ago, because they trample on the modesty of the person being interviewed. And that is the best case.

What almost nobody looks at is the apparatus. A fixed hour, a chair that never moves, and a single rule: say whatever comes, without correcting it. The analyst does not advise. Nor does he console, or talk about himself. And there is a silence that nobody rushes to fill — that, more than anything else.

That is the Freud of method. And he is the one who still works.

What the frame does

The technical word is frame — in Spanish, encuadre. It sounds bureaucratic, and that is why it gets underestimated: it looks like the administrative part of treatment, the schedule and the fee. It isn't.

The frame does one thing, and it is enormous. It holds a space where patients can hear themselves without anyone rushing to cover up the noise.

Because that is what I see happening almost everywhere, inside and outside the consulting room: the moment someone becomes uncomfortable, we step in. We explain, we reassure, we look on the bright side. Uncomfortable presence has come to be seen as something bad, almost as a failure on the part of whoever is accompanying. In the practice of psychology, I am convinced it isn't.

The frame blocks that reflex. It forbids the analyst to console, to give opinions, to give advice. And in that void the only thing that matters happens: the person begins to speak to no one, and at some point hears themselves.

The breakthrough cannot be forced. No interpretation drags it out, however brilliant. It arrives when there is a frame and there is time, and when someone has had the patience to stay quiet.

The therapist does not produce that moment. He only holds the conditions for it to happen.

A Viennese temperament

It is hard to separate that method from the place where it was born.

Freud was Viennese, a subject of the Austro-Hungarian empire, and from the autumn of 1902 he gathered a small group every Wednesday in his apartment at Berggasse 19 — at first barely four physicians — to discuss psychology and neuropathology. It was the Wednesday Psychological Society, which in 1908 was reconstituted as the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society.

And one detail says more than any theory: the meetings began at a fixed hour, half past eight in the evening, after dinner, and names were drawn from an urn: whoever came up had to speak, so that no one could take over the discussion. A gathering with a frame. The frame before the frame.

The after-dinner part keeps me thinking. A full belly, a happy heart — or a full belly, a happy brain? They would know. Had it happened in Peru, with our portions, I dare say human thought would have been deciphered long ago. Forgive the gluttony.

Around it breathed a tradition that had already cast doubt on the rational subject. Schopenhauer had said that the will rules the intellect. Nietzsche had dismantled the self's sincerity with itself. Freud said he long avoided reading Nietzsche, not for the sake of priority, he explained, but to keep his impartial position — and still the echoes are there. It is not far-fetched to think that that Central European air, formal and wary of enthusiasm, made the rigour of the frame feel almost natural.

Other traditions sought the same thing by other roads.

The basement

Reading Dostoevsky is nothing like drinking sweet tea. Crime and Punishment does not keep the reader company: it puts you inside the head of a man who has already killed and leaves you there. The Brothers Karamazov argues about God without siding with anyone. Tolstoy has Ivan Ilyich die for a hundred pages and offers no comfort until the end, and barely then.

That basement has a key straight to the abyss. And going down is uncomfortable but also, it must be said, delicious — that Russian mix of depth and vodka where the descent is both a challenge and a party. Nietzsche warned that if you gaze long into the abyss, the abyss gazes back. In the Russian version, it also buys you a drink.

Truth there does not arrive by method. It arrives by immersion: you go down and stay until the basement speaks. In the best of cases, the one who comes up is no longer the one who went down.

That form shows in Russian psychology too. Vygotsky came to the discipline from literature: as a very young man he wrote theatre criticism for provincial newspapers and an essay on Hamlet, and his 1925 doctoral thesis was a Psychology of Art that analyses fables, a story by Bunin, and closes its analysis with Hamlet. Luria, the great Soviet neuropsychologist, championed what he called romantic science: instead of reducing patients to statistics, he wrote two complete cases, about a man who could not forget and about a soldier who had lost his world to a bullet wound. They read like novels: Oliver Sacks recalled reading the first thirty pages of the first one thinking it was one, and he recognised Luria as a master precisely for that.

And here a circle closes that I did not expect to find. In a letter to Sacks, Luria explained why he preferred that style: because he was against the formal statistical approach and in favour of the qualitative study of the person. And that Luria had started with Freud: at nineteen he founded a psychoanalytic society in Kazan and received a letter from Freud himself, and in Moscow he was scientific secretary of the Russian Psychoanalytic Society until Freud was effectively banned in the Soviet Union in 1930.

The uncomfortable part has to be said, because it is the same house: Russia also gave us Pavlov and reflexology, the most mechanistic thing psychology ever produced. The same culture held both the abyss and the reflex. That tension invalidates nothing; it is part of the character.

Bakhtin, reading Dostoevsky, gave the difference with Vienna a name: the polyphonic. The characters are autonomous consciousnesses and none has the last word, not even the author. In the Viennese frame, by contrast, there is a frame and a deliberate asymmetry. One speaks and the other stays silent, and that is agreed upon.

The mud

The North American tradition has depth too, but of another kind. It does not need to descend into hell and walk through it to draw a reflection. It found that chaos already lives in what is human, in what is torn, in broad daylight.

Hemingway looks for it in war and in the body: what one does, what one drinks, how one endures. Capote writes In Cold Blood by spending years inside a real crime until literature and case file become indistinguishable. Twain tells, with infinite tenderness, the adventures — and at times the miseries — of a boy on a river. And Harper Lee builds all of To Kill a Mockingbird on a metaphor that needs no explanation: the mockingbirds are the innocent. In the end it is the most innocent of all, Boo Radley, who kills to save two children — though the novel never says so, it only lets you understand. And the sheriff invents that Bob Ewell fell on his own knife. Not to cover up a crime: to protect from public exposure a man who had not left his house in years. A frame, again, built so that someone can go on being who they are.

There is no metaphysical basement. There are facts, and one has to answer for them.

In Chicago that became a school. The functionalism of Dewey and Angell stopped asking what consciousness is and started asking what it is for. Behind it stood James's pragmatism: true is what works. From there to behaviour, to measurement and to outcome-oriented therapy there is a straight line.

And an irony worth not hiding: at that same university, between 1945 and 1957, Carl Rogers helped establish the university's counseling center, and there he researched and developed client-centred therapy and unconditional acceptance. The house of the grounded argument also gave birth to the psychology of warm companionship. Traditions are never pure blocks.

The French who do not console

The strangest bridge is the one that closes best.

France is a country of contrasts that never lets go of any of them: Napoleon and the Bastille, the monarchy and the guillotine, all living together in the same memory. A place where dichotomies are not resolved; they accumulate. I am not a historian and will not pretend to be one, but something of that habit of living between opposites without forcing them to reconcile shows in its philosophy.

Camus does not say that the absurd hides a meaning, or that your pain is special. He says the world is indifferent and that one must imagine Sisyphus happy. Sartre does not relieve you: he hands your freedom back to you as a sentence, makes you responsible, and calls bad faith the excuses you invent so as not to see it.

Neither of them validates. Neither of them chases you with life's misery so that you feel it more. They leave you facing yourself with the discomfort intact.

That is, exactly, the position the frame asks of the therapist. And it is the opposite of the psychology that promises that everything has a purpose.

And it is not a whim of mine. There is lineage. The Swiss psychiatrist Ludwig Binswanger founded existential analysis — Daseinsanalyse — around 1930, drawing on Heidegger, to stop reducing the patient to a set of symptoms and drives. Medard Boss later formalised it, working directly with Heidegger. Frankl developed logotherapy; Yalom, later and in the United States, formulated the four ultimate concerns: death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness.

What is remarkable is Binswanger's relationship with Freud. He was one of the Swiss in the Freudian group Jung led in Zurich, criticised Freudian metapsychology without mercy — and kept a friendship and a correspondence with Freud that lasted decades. They disagreed all their lives without ever breaking. The conversation between the frame and existentialism has been open for a century.

What converges

It is not about which tradition is better.

The Russian goes down to the basement and stays there, unhurried, until the basement speaks. The American does not go down: he finds chaos where it already was, in broad daylight, and asks what we do tomorrow. And the Frenchman withdraws consolation, which is, at bottom, the most brutal operation of the three.

All three go to the same place: a moment in which a person meets themselves without intermediaries.

What the Freud of method did was more modest. He did not go down to the basement or propose a programme. He built a room with an hour, a chair and a rule. Then he waited.

That is the best Freud. Not the one who was right about sex. The one who understood that certain truths only appear when nobody is chasing them.

The classroom: as an undistinguished student

I write this from the inside, and out of step: I began studying psychology at thirty-nine, with forty just around the corner, after having read almost everyone I have just named. That gives an uncomfortable perspective. You see the curriculum from outside and from inside at the same time.

What is taught is content. Attachment theories, stages, syndromes, protocols. And a growing layer of performance: activities whose teaching goal is to lose your shyness, loosen up, expose yourself. None of that is wrong in itself.

But how many hours are devoted to silence?

Not silence as a pause between two questions, but silence as an instrument: not filling it, tolerating the emptiness of a session where nothing seems to happen, resisting the urge to intervene. It is one of the hardest things in the craft, and it barely appears in the curriculum.

Nor are we taught to withstand hard criticism. And there is a tradition — that of the grounded argument, the one Chicago turned into method — in which an unsupported opinion does not count as an answer. It hurts to be told so that plainly. But it teaches, and above all it makes you responsible: whoever is going to hold another person's process does not voice opinions; they take on burdens.

Empathy as slogan or as burden

There is a phrase repeated ad nauseam in classrooms, conferences and consulting rooms: you have to be empathetic. And a stronger version, which I have heard from professors, students and practising professionals: there is no such thing as bad empathy.

There is.

Holding someone's mental health is not hugging them and understanding them. Empathy without distance stops being a resource and becomes a risk: when the therapist gets so involved that they mistake the patient's pain for their own, they lose the place from which to accompany. That has a technical name and more than a century of literature behind it, and yet what gets taught is the slogan, not the limit.

What sustains a process is less warm and more demanding: to accompany without merging, to keep the distance, to watch the transference, to give grounds when it is due and to stay silent when it is not.

Asymmetry begins before the greeting

And there is something that is almost never said and that seems central to me.

A healthy session begins with an asymmetry. Not hierarchy: asymmetry of position, which is something else. One of the two came to speak and the other is there to hold the frame. That has to be clear, and it is not declared.

It is inferred. From how one introduces oneself. From how one greets. From how one speaks. From how one looks.

The frame does not begin when the patient sits down. It begins in the corridor, in the tone in which their name is spoken, in the clothes, in the session starting at the time it was said it would start. All of that communicates, before the first word, whether this is a conversation between two acquaintances or a room built for something else.

That is exactly what those schools understood and what we ought to recover.

Closing

Of everything that is taught, what is taught least is how to build the room.

We learn stages. Attachment, trauma, protocols, syndromes. We learn to name what is happening to someone and hand it back to them in order, which is no small thing. What almost nobody teaches is how to stay still while the other person finds, on their own, what they came to find.

Discomfort, if you let it, tells a story.
